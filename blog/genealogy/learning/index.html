The genealogy of language models

August 11, 2026

How do models actually learn?

In the previous section, we built the model and hopefully grounded our intuition to understand the choices made and their raison d'être. What should be abundantly clear, however, is that nothing in that construction tells us how the model learns, nor why it learns. Nothing in that construction knows any language. WQ,WK,WVW^Q,W^K,W^VWQ,WK,WV do not begin knowing what a noun is. The embedding for dog is not close to the embedding for puppy. No attention head has been assigned the job of resolving pronouns. The feed-forward networks contain no handwritten rules of grammar.

Surprisingly, the motivation for the model to learn is essentially

predict the correct token.

For the original Transformer, the one created for translation, we add some specificity:

given the source sentence and the correct target prefix, predict the next target token.

Everything else follows from repeatedly measuring how wrong the model was and changing its parameters so that, next time, the correct token receives slightly more probability, and is thus more likely to be chosen. Before we begin this discussion, however, it is worth backtracking and clarifying a term I have been using loosely for an entire post.

Tokens

We mentioned previously that the model takes an input sequence of symbol representations xxx. xxx is not the words that compose the sentence, but instead integers produced by a tokenizer that converts text into a sequence drawn from some finite vocabulary

V={1,…,V}.\mathcal{V} = \{1, \ldots, V\}.V={1,…,V}.

Using an example sentence

"The transformer unpacks unhelpful words into smaller pieces before processing them."

the GPT-5.x Tokenizer splits it into 15 tokens, namely

The transformer unpacks unhelpful words into smaller pieces before processing them.

whose corresponding token IDs are

[976,59595,537,103878,537,11283,1500,6391,1511,13679,12762,2254,12323,1373,13].[976, 59595, 537, 103878, 537, 11283, 1500, 6391, 1511, 13679, 12762, 2254, 12323, 1373, 13].[976,59595,537,103878,537,11283,1500,6391,1511,13679,12762,2254,12323,1373,13].

The number of tokens matches neither the number of words nor the number of characters; tokens are sub-word.

Why sub-word?

Language is open vocabulary; in that it is not restricted to a predefined set of words or phrases; names, compounds, spelling variations, technical terminology, and newly invented words make it impossible to maintain a reasonably sized vocabulary containing every word one might encounter. So, unless we are willing to constantly document and tokenize every new word and its spelling variations from all across the globe, it is just untenable as tokenizing approach.

Character-level tokenization also doesn't make much sense. The sentence above is 83 characters and 15 tokens. Since ordinary self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, a character-level tokenizer would ask for roughly thirty times the attention work to say the same thing.

Sennrich, Haddow, and BirchCitationSennrich et al. Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units. 2015. PDF had shown that byte-pair encoding could be adapted to neural machine translation by decomposing uncommon words into reusable sub-word units. A frequent word might remain a single token; a rare word might become several smaller tokens. This provided a fixed vocabulary without requiring every possible word to have its own entry.

The remaining choice is where to sit between the two failures. A larger vocabulary represents more strings per token and shortens sequences, but enlarges the embedding and output matrices. A smaller vocabulary shrinks those matrices and lengthens sequences, and length is the expensive axis.

Sub-word tokenization is a compromise nobody would have designed on purpose, and it is still what everything runs on. It will eventually become an engineering problem of its own, and I suspect a post of its own. For now, assume some tokenizer has handed us a sequence of token IDs and has been thanked for its trouble.

Weight tying

As described, we have an embedding matrix

E∈RV×dmodelE \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d_{\text{model}}}E∈RV×dmodel​

that maps tokens to vectors, and an "output" matrix

Wout∈RV×dmodelW_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d_{\text{model}}}Wout​∈RV×dmodel​

that scores hidden vectors against possible output tokens. With V=37,000V = 37{,}000V=37,000 and dmodel=512d_{\text{model}} = 512dmodel​=512, each is about 191919 million parameters. The base model has 656565 million in total, so keeping two independent copies would take the model to roughly 848484 million and place close to half of it in two eventual learned "lookup" tables.

This, I believe, is more than enough to consider grouping these matrices into one. With that being said, there is also a conceptual argument that has enough merit and is the reason I am talking about this in the first place. I ask you, what does a row in mean in these matrices? The row EvE_vEv​ answers

what vector should represent token vvv when I read it?

while (Wout)v(W_{\text{out}})_v(Wout​)v​ answers

what direction in representation space should make me believe the answer is token vvv?

Press and WolfCitationOfir Press, Lior Wolf. Using the Output Embedding to Improve Language Models. 2016. PDF showed that the output matrix of a neural language model already behaves as a meaningful word embedding in its own right, and that tying the input and output matrices could improve perplexity while substantially reducing parameter count. In neural translation models their experiments found that tying reduced model size dramatically without degrading performance. Inan, Khosravi, and Socher arrived independently at closely related weight-sharing results from a loss-based analysis.CitationInan, Khosravi, and Socher. Tying Word Vectors and Word Classifiers: A Loss Framework for Language Modeling. 2016. PDF

We therefore impose

Wout=E,W_{\text{out}} = E,Wout​=E,

and with our convention that vocabulary embeddings occupy the rows of EEE, logits become

ℓt=Eht.\ell_t = Eh_t.ℓt​=Eht​.

Read that way, a logit is an inner product between the current hidden state and the embedding of a candidate token. Prediction becomes a similarity search in the same space that reading uses. The original Transformer goes one step further and shares a single matrix across both embedding layers and the pre-softmax transformation, which is commonly called three-way weight tying.

Weight tying reduces capacity in exchange for parameter efficiency and an inductive bias that says these two problems ought to share structure. There is no mathematical necessity that the best space for interpreting an input token is the best space for classifying an output token. Tying asserts that it is, on the strength of a plausible story and a better perplexity number.

We can now read symbols, transform them, and turn the final representations into probabilities. We have no definition of being wrong.

The objective

Let x=⟨x1,…,xn⟩x = \langle x_1, \ldots, x_n \ranglex=⟨x1​,…,xn​⟩ be the source sentence and y=⟨y1,…,ym⟩y = \langle y_1, \ldots, y_m \rangley=⟨y1​,…,ym​⟩ be the target translation.   1. The magnitude is bounded. However catastrophically wrong the model is, no logit gradient exceeds 111 in absolute value. The loss itself is unbounded above, but the signal it emits is not.
  2. The gradient sums to zero: ∑j(pj−qj)=1−1=0\sum_j (p_j - q_j) = 1 - 1 = 0∑j​(pj​−qj​)=1−1=0. Probability at this layer is conserved, so the correct token cannot be raised without something else being lowered. Learning here is redistribution.

Consider the alternative. An untrained model produces nonsense. Feed that nonsense back as context and later predictions are conditioned on increasingly meaningless prefixes, so the model spends its first several thousand steps learning the correct continuations of sequences that appear nowhere in any language. Teacher forcing keeps the trajectory anchored to real examples, at a price we are about to pay.

It also combines particularly well with causal masking. Since every correct previous token is known, the entire shifted target can enter the decoder at once, and a sentence containing mmm yields roughly mmm supervised next-token problems for the price of one forward pass.

Training & Inference

During training, when predicting token ttt, the model receives y<ty_{< t}y<t​, the correct prefix, irrespective of what it would have chosen. During inferenceDefinitionInference is the process of using an already trained large language model to generate text or answers for new user prompts. it receives y^<t\hat y_{< t}y^​<t​, its own generated prefix, which may be wrong.

This train-test mismatch became known as exposure bias. Bengio et al.CitationBengio et al. Scheduled Sampling for Sequence Prediction with Recurrent Neural Networks. 2015. PDF proposed scheduled sampling: gradually replace some ground-truth previous tokens during training with tokens generated by the model itself, thereby exposing the model to the kinds of prefixes it will encounter at inference.

Huszár then showed that the scheduled-sampling objective is not, in general, a consistent estimator of the data distribution.CitationHuszár. How (not) to Train your Generative Model. 2015. PDF The fix changes which distribution the model is incentivized to learn.

RemarkBeing pedantic

It is common to say "teacher forcing causes exposure bias, therefore maximum likelihood is flawed."

The autoregressive factorization is exact. If every learned conditional matched the true conditional distribution, sampling autoregressively from the model would reproduce the correct joint distribution.

The practical problem is that our learned conditionals are imperfect. During rollout, an imperfect prediction may place us in regions of sequence space where those approximations are worse, allowing errors to compound.

So there is a genuine train-generation mismatch, but simply replacing maximum likelihood with model-generated prefixes does not automatically solve it.

This returns much later under other names and other training paradigms. For the 2017 Transformer we stick with teacher-forced maximum likelihood.

How does loss reach attention heads?

Return to the example sentence from the previous section, "Because the trophy was too large for the suitcase, it did not fit." Suppose one attention head eventually learns that it should retrieve information from trophy. Who told it to? Nobody. The only supervision available was that, several layers later, the model assigned insufficient probability to the correct translated token. Backpropagation is what carries that fact backwards.DefinitionBackpropagation is the repeated application of the chain rule over a computation graph, computing the gradient of a scalar loss with respect to every intermediate value and parameter in a single backward pass.

Consider a single attention operation Z=AVZ = AVZ=AV, where

A=softmax⁡(QK⊤dk),A = \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right),A=softmax(dk​​QK⊤​),

and suppose the gradient arriving from later layers is

G=∂L∂Z.G = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial Z}.G=∂Z∂L​.

Then

∂L∂V=A⊤G,\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial V} = A^\top G,∂V∂L​=A⊤G,

so the forward attention weights also determine where learning signal is routed backward through the value pathway. A position that was ignored in the forward pass receives almost no gradient. Similarly,

∂L∂A=GV⊤.\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial A} = GV^\top.∂A∂L​=GV⊤.

The (i,j)(i,j)(i,j) entry is ⟨gi,vj⟩\langle g_i, v_j \rangle⟨gi​,vj​⟩: the inner product between the direction position iii's output should have moved and the content sitting at position jjj. If the information at jjj points the way the loss wanted to go, the gradient asks for more attention on jjj. Credit assignment across positions is itself a similarity computation, over the same vectors the forward pass used.

That error then propagates backward through the softmax and through QK⊤QK^\topQK⊤, producing gradients for QQQ and KKK and ultimately for WQW^QWQ, WKW^KWK, and WVW^VWV. Since those were themselves computed from earlier hidden representations, the gradient continues through every preceding block to the embeddings. The whole path:

  1. The error appears at the output. At each target position the loss measures how much probability the model failed to assign to the correct next token.
  2. We differentiate through the softmax, starting from ∂L/∂ℓj=pj−qj\partial \mathcal L / \partial \ell_j = p_j - q_j∂L/∂ℓj​=pj​−qj​.
  3. We pass through the output projection. Since ℓt=Woutht\ell_t = W_{\text{out}}h_tℓt​=Wout​ht​, the logit gradients say how the final hidden state should have differed. Under weight tying, this step also contributes to the gradient of the shared matrix.
  4. We pass through the decoder block. Feed-forward network, residual connections, layer normalization, cross-attention, and masked self-attention each receive a signal describing how changing their output would have changed the loss.
  5. Attention splits the signal in two. Gradients flow through the retrieved content VVV and through the weights AAA, so the model learns both what to carry and where to look.
  6. The projections are updated. If making it attend more strongly to trophy would have raised the probability of the correct output token, the parameters governing that query-key compatibility move in exactly that direction.
  7. The gradient keeps going, recursively through earlier blocks and into the embeddings, so tokens useful in similar contexts can, over many examples, acquire related structure.

What we are left with is ∇θL\nabla_\theta \mathcal L∇θ​L: a locally valid description of how the loss would change if each parameter were perturbed. No attention head was ever told that this pronoun refers to trophy. A single scalar at the end of the network became millions of local redistribution signals.

Backpropagation gives the direction in which each parameter affects the loss. It says nothing at all about how far to move.

How does it improve?

We do not update the network after a single token, nor after a single sentence.

Training uses minibatches. The English-German experiment used about 4.54.54.5 million sentence pairs and the English-French dataset about 363636 million. Sentence pairs of similar length were grouped together, with each batch holding roughly 25,00025{,}00025,000 source tokens and 25,00025{,}00025,000 target tokens.NoteSequences in a batch need compatible tensor shapes, so shorter ones are padded; a batch containing lengths 10, 12, 15 and 500 pads the short examples nearly to 500 and wastes an enormous amount of computation. Batching similar lengths avoids paying for padding.

The paper describes batch size in tokens, not in sentences. A batch of 100 ten-token sequences has very different computational cost from a batch of 100 thousand-token sequences. This distinction will eventually become standard operating procedure for large-scale training.

Warmup

A learning rate determines how aggressively we trust the current gradient. Too small, and learning proceeds painfully slowly. Too large, and we overshoot useful regions of parameter space or destabilize optimization entirely.

Why warm up at all? The paper offers the schedule as obvious and derives none of it, but later work supplies an illuminating piece of hindsight. Recall from the previous section that the original Transformer normalizes after the residual addition,

LayerNorm⁡(x+SubLayer⁡(x)),\operatorname{LayerNorm}(x + \operatorname{SubLayer}(x)),LayerNorm(x+SubLayer(x)),

and that I promised to explain why later architectures reverse this. Xiong et al.CitationXiong et al. On Layer Normalization in the Transformer Architecture. 2020. PDF analyzed Transformer optimization and argued that post-norm Transformers can have unusually large gradients near the output layers at initialization. Applying a large learning rate immediately to those gradients destabilizes training; warmup buys an initial period of small, cautious updates instead. The same analysis explains why pre-norm Transformers are substantially easier to optimize, and why warmup stopped being load-bearing once the normalization moved.

Which is to say: the schedule was compensating for the architecture.

A sufficiently expressive network can lower training loss by learning peculiarities of the training set that do not generalize. Regularization is, in a sense, deliberately making the training problem harder in the hope that the solution becomes more general.

The Transformer employs one more, rather more interesting, instance of this philosophy.

Label smoothing

The cross-entropy target so far has been brutally certain. If token yyy is correct, then qy=1q_y = 1qy​=1 and qj=0q_j = 0qj​=0 for every j≠yj \ne yj=y, which asserts that the training data contains no uncertainty whatsoever. The model is rewarded for driving py→1p_y \rightarrow 1py​→1 and every pj→0p_j \rightarrow 0pj​→0, and at no point is it told that being 95%95\%95% confident would have been quite enough.

Contextualize this point with regards to the motivating problem for the Transformer architecture: translation. A translated sentence has several acceptable words to choose from that retain the meaning of the original sentence. The one-hot target insists the other words are worth nothing. We have been training the model to be certain about something that isn't and likely will not be certain.

Szegedy et al.CitationSzegedy et al. Rethinking the Inception Architecture for Computer Vision. 2015. PDF introduced label smoothing to discourage that extreme. Rather than train against the one-hot qqq, we mix it with a background distribution uuu:

A complete training step

We can finally assemble the procedure. Take a minibatch of source-target sentence pairs, and then:

  1. Tokenize. Raw strings become discrete sequences x1,…,xnx_1,\ldots,x_nx1​,…,xn​ and y1,…,ymy_1,\ldots,y_my1​,…,ym​.
  2. Embed. Each token retrieves a learned vector et=Exte_t = E_{x_t}et​=Ext​​, scaled by dmodel\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}dmodel​​ and combined with positional information.
  3. Encode the source. The encoder repeatedly applies self-attention and feed-forward transformations, producing contextual source representations.
  4. Shift the target right. The decoder receives ⟨BOS⟩,y1,…,ym−1\langle\mathrm{BOS}\rangle, y_1, \ldots, y_{m-1}⟨BOS⟩,y1​,…,ym−1​, and causal masking ensures each position may use only its correct prefix.
  5. Run the decoder. Masked self-attention communicates among earlier target positions; cross-attention retrieves from the encoded source; the feed-forward layers transform what results.
  6. Project to logits. ℓt=Eht\ell_t = Eh_tℓt​=Eht​, under weight tying.
  7. Normalize into probabilities. pt=softmax⁡(ℓt)p_t = \operatorname{softmax}(\ell_t)pt​=softmax(ℓt​).
  8. Compare against the correct next tokens. With label smoothing, L=−∑t∑vqt,v′log⁡pt,v\mathcal{L} = -\sum_t\sum_v q'_{t,v}\log p_{t,v}L=−∑t​∑v​qt,v′​logpt,v​.
  9. Backpropagate. Compute ∇θL\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}∇θ​L for every trainable parameter: E,WQ,WK,WV,WO,W1,W2,γ,βE, W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O, W_1, W_2, \gamma, \betaE,WQ,WK,WV,WO,W1​,W2​,γ,β, and the rest.
  10. Update with Adam, using the scheduled learning rate.

Then do it again, a hundred thousand times. Twelve hours later you have a translator, and not one line of the procedure above mentioned translation.

What has actually been learned?

Almost nothing we discussed in the architecture section is directly supervised. Nobody labels antecedent heads, syntax heads, useful embedding dimensions, or feed-forward features. There is no Lsyntax\mathcal{L}_{\text{syntax}}Lsyntax​, no Lfacts\mathcal{L}_{\text{facts}}Lfacts​, no Lreasoning\mathcal{L}_{\text{reasoning}}Lreasoning​. The only externally supplied signal is, in effect, given this context, you should have assigned more probability to this token. Every piece of internal structure exists because it happened to reduce that quantity.

Which does, in fairness, explain a great deal. To predict

The capital of France is ___

it is useful to encode facts about France. To predict

Because the trophy was too large for the suitcase, it did not ___

it is useful to represent which object could fit inside which. To predict source code, it is useful to infer syntax and program structure. To predict an argument, it may be useful to represent relationships that look suspiciously like reasoning.

That is simultaneously the extraordinary power and the extraordinary limitation of the objective: the model is rewarded for whatever internal machinery helps prediction, and not at all for discovering machinery we would find intelligible. Whether these representations deserve the names we give them, and how far next-token prediction can push such abilities, are entirely separate questions—ones we will have to return to when we reach interpretability.

At every position we ask the same question, what comes next?, measure the error, differentiate it, and adjust several million numbers. Then we ask again.

The next step in the genealogy was to ask what happens if we stop treating it as a translation system at all: throw away the encoder, discard cross-attention, point the decoder at an enormous pile of unlabelled text, and make next-token prediction itself the entire pretraining task.
